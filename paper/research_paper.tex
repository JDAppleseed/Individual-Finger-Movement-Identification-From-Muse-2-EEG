\begin{abstract}
Consumer-grade EEG headsets offer an accessible path to motor-assistive brain-computer interfaces, but their low channel count, high noise floor, and indirect coupling to hand kinematics make finger-level decoding an open challenge. For individuals who cannot use EMG-based systems due to muscle damage, nerve injury, or other neuromuscular conditions, no consumer EEG alternative has demonstrated deployment-gated finger-level accuracy and replay evidence for practical assistive-control development. AlphaHand is a low-cost Muse 2 pipeline that achieves, for the first time in consumer EEG, subject-specific finger-level command decoding relevant to fine-motor assistive access. The pipeline records timestamp-aligned EEG with event markers, extracts 0.250 s windows stepped at 0.05 s, and trains a calibrated multi-head CNN+LSTM classifier evaluated through the same command gates used for downstream robotic-hand actuation. The classification task spans 11 classes---five fingers (thumb through pinky) each labeled across two movement states (open and close), plus a shared REST class. The featured subject-specific deployment checkpoint (2-M16), trained on two distinct movement sessions and one auxiliary REST session, achieved 89.79\% held-out action accuracy, 85.96\% deployment-consistent finger accuracy, and 84.66\% joint action--finger accuracy under deterministic evaluation. In pseudo-live replay across 3324 emitted positive windows, the command path reached 95.37\% non-REST precision and detected 91.11\% of 596 movement events at 83 ms median first-command latency; window-level send recall of 31.56\% reflects stability gating and command-policy behavior, with classifier error captured separately in the held-out window metrics. False actuation during REST was rare, occurring on only 6 of 2404 (0.25\%) true REST windows. Prospective multi-subject testing is the next validation step. Note: this testing will happen before full publishing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Millions of people in the United States live with limb loss or congenital limb difference. Recent analyses estimate the affected population exceeds 5.6 million, including nearly 2.3 million with limb loss and 3.4 million with congenital difference \cite{Cain2025AmputeeCare}. Limb amputation carries substantial psychological burden alongside its physical consequences; a 2024 meta-analysis estimated pooled depression prevalence after amputation at 33.85\% (95\% CI: 27.15--40.54\%) \cite{Singh2024Depression}. Restoring meaningful hand function for this population requires more than binary grasp detection: many daily tasks depend on which finger moves and when, making finger-level intent decoding a demanding and clinically meaningful target.

EMG is the standard control modality for practical upper-limb prostheses \cite{Scheme2011EMG}. It is effective when informative residual musculature is available. For users with limited or absent peripheral control signals, consumer EEG can make neural control viable where residual musculature cannot support EMG, opening an access path independent of peripheral motor signals. Low-channel EEG is spatially blurred and sensitive to motion and impedance variation. Achieving finger-level decoding under these constraints is therefore more demanding, and the results are correspondingly more significant. AlphaHand demonstrates that a low-cost, low-channel system can preserve enough usable intent structure to support a conservative assistive-control command path. Finger-level decoding from such hardware therefore represents a stringent demonstration that low-cost brain-computer interfaces \cite{Wolpaw2002BCI} can move beyond coarse command vocabularies.

Prior EEG work has shown that classification performance improves substantially with deep architectures, spanning approaches from shallow features to end-to-end learned representations \cite{Lotte2018Review}, including the Deep ConvNet family and the compact EEGNet architecture \cite{Schirrmeister2017Deep,Lawhern2018EEGNet}. At the task level, noninvasive studies have demonstrated that individual finger movements can be discriminated from EEG under controlled conditions \cite{Liao2014FingerEEG,Mohamed2011WristFinger}, and Xiao and Ding further showed that spectral-structure features from high-density EEG carry discriminative information for individual fingers beyond classic mu/beta features \cite{XiaoDing2015FingerSpectral}. More recently, Ding et al. demonstrated real-time noninvasive robotic hand control at individual finger level using 128-channel EEG in experienced BCI users \cite{Ding2025FingerRoboticControl}. These results establish that EEG contains task-relevant finger information. AlphaHand extends that evidence into the constrained deployment setting: high-density sensing is replaced by a four-channel consumer headset validated for research use \cite{Krigolson2017Muse,Cannard2021Muse}, and predictions remain reportable after calibration, safety gates, and replay through the command path. Because transfer across users remains a persistent challenge in EEG-BCI, subject-specific deployment is the appropriate first validation target \cite{Wu2022Transfer}. Additionally, once a classifier is connected to hardware, calibration and uncertainty become major operational concerns \cite{Gal2016Dropout,Guo2017Calibration}.

AlphaHand answers the central deployment question by showing that subject-specific structure survives calibration, safety gating, and replay through an auditable command path. This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item An end-to-end consumer EEG pipeline for finger-level command decoding, spanning Muse 2 acquisition, timestamp-aligned labeling, fixed-window extraction, subject-specific CNN+LSTM training, calibration, deterministic evaluation, and optional robotic-hand actuation.
\item A deployment-consistent evaluation protocol that reports held-out accuracy alongside command-path precision, event hit rate, latency, and false REST actuation under pseudo-live replay.
\item A channel-ablation analysis showing which Muse electrodes preserve subject-specific decoding performance, with temporal channels identified as the primary carriers of usable signal.
\item A provenance account for the featured 2-M16 result, including session merging, REST pruning, window counts, saved run outputs, and the full traceable evidence chain.
\end{itemize}
The March 19, 2026 2-M16 bundle is the featured subject-specific deployment result throughout.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Pipeline Overview}

Acquisition records raw Muse samples with event markers; window extraction converts recordings into labeled fixed-length examples; training saves model checkpoints and calibrated test predictions; deterministic evaluation computes metrics and command-path summaries; calibration review generates reliability and uncertainty outputs; live inference optionally sends gated commands to the robotic hand; and post-live review converts prediction logs into auditable segments. \subsection{Data Acquisition and Persistence}

\subsubsection{Human Subjects and Ethics}

Recordings were collected under IRB approval permitting enrollment of participants ages 16 and older. All participants provided written consent or assent; written parent or guardian consent was obtained for minors. The featured 2-M16 participant was a 16-year-old study-team self-subject. The broader project archive contains recordings from approximately 20 consented participants, ages 16--60, male and female, including students, teachers, and parents. This manuscript analyzes the featured 2-M16 deployment bundle because it contains the complete saved dataset, checkpoint, calibration, and replay evidence chain.

\subsubsection{Hardware}

The data analyzed here were acquired with a Muse 2 headset (Muse/InteraXon Inc., Toronto, ON, Canada), configured as a four-channel wearable EEG system at \TargetFsHz~Hz \cite{Muse2Specs,Muse2Official}. The four electrodes (TP9, AF7, AF8, and TP10) place AF7 and AF8 over anterior-frontal sites and TP9 and TP10 over lateral temporal/periauricular sites within the international EEG placement system; Bird et al. provide a placement diagram relative to standard EEG landmarks \cite{Bird2019MusePlacement}. Figure~\ref{fig:muse-montage} also marks Fpz as the frontal reference point used to orient the headset montage. This hardware defines the central constraint of the study: finger-level decoding from a consumer headset, operating without the central motor coverage of a research-grade cap \cite{Krigolson2017Muse,Cannard2021Muse}.

\subsubsection{EEG Artifact-Minimizing Recording Protocol}

During recording, subjects were seated, relaxed, and instructed to keep gaze fixed on the task point while avoiding non-task movements. The operator monitored stream health, electrode connectivity, and general system status live, with breaks used when needed. These controls were designed to reduce motion, gaze, and connectivity-related artifacts at recording time; artifact limitations are addressed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Streaming Model}

All EEG samples are timestamped in the LSL clock domain \cite{Kothe2025LSL} to prevent drift across devices and sessions. Event timestamps are converted into the same timebase before labeling. Absolute session time is defined as:

\[
t_{\text{session}} = t_{\text{lsl}} - t_{\text{stream\_start}}
\]

\subsubsection{Persistence Architecture}

Raw EEG samples are stored first as NumPy shards to preserve sample fidelity and avoid rounding error from CSV serialization. Event markers are written to both a legacy CSV view and an authoritative \texttt{events.jsonl} file. The analysis in this paper uses the JSONL event log as the canonical label source.

\subsection{Window Extraction and Leakage Controls}

Sliding windows are extracted with fixed duration \WindowSec~s and hop \HopSec~s, then resampled to a fixed \TargetFsHz~Hz grid. Each window receives one action label and, when appropriate, one finger label. The deployable label space separates action from finger identity: a 5-way active-finger head is trained on non-REST windows, while a binary applicability head predicts whether a finger label is meaningful for the current window. Windows labeled with an active action (OPEN or CLOSE) but carrying finger NONE are rejected during extraction before downstream training or evaluation. Figure~\ref{fig:raw-windowing} grounds the procedure with an event-centered recorded segment and the corresponding overlapping extraction windows.

\[
N = f_s \times T_{\text{window}} = \TargetFsHz \times \WindowSec = 64~\text{samples}
\]

Windows containing non-finite values are excluded during training. The extracted bundles also store extraction artifact flags and gap flags; the corresponding counts are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dataset}.

\subsubsection{Split Strategy and Leakage Controls}
A small number of REST events can still generate many overlapping REST windows under the sliding extraction scheme; REST window counts in the evaluation tables reflect this windowing effect. Training uses a group-aware 80/20 holdout split as recorded in each run configuration. The pipeline asserts no group overlap between train and test windows, supports optional temporal purging, and runs a window-index diagnostic to detect suspicious ordering leakage. In the representative saved outputs, the group-aware split is applied without an additional temporal exclusion band around the test windows.

The saved window bundles include trial and block identifier fields, but in the current outputs both are constant across each bundle. Strict trial-level evaluation is therefore unavailable for the runs reported here. The split logic instead relies primarily on event identifiers and secondary time-based grouping for windows with no event ID. Windows without event IDs arise primarily from REST/background intervals or boundary regions not assigned to a discrete movement event; these windows are grouped by secondary time-based identifiers so that temporally adjacent unlabeled-background windows remain together within a single split.

A post-hoc audit of the cached test indices found no shared (\texttt{session\_id}, \texttt{event\_id}) groups between test windows and the complementary training/calibration pool, and zero exact duplicate raw windows crossing the train/test boundary. This reduces the risk of direct split leakage; temporal dependence from overlapping windows is a known property of the windowing scheme, addressed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Diagnostics and Quality Controls}
The pipeline includes explicit hardening against common streaming-EEG failure modes: non-finite windows are removed before training, group-aware splitting prevents direct group overlap, optional purging can remove windows adjacent in time to test data, and a separate validation stage produces a dataset-quality report for each run.

\subsection{Model and Training}

AlphaHand uses a multi-head CNN+LSTM network with shared temporal features and three prediction heads: a 5-way active-finger head, a 3-way action head, and a binary finger-applicability head. The full architecture is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:model-config}. Two temporal 1D convolution blocks (16 and 32 channels; kernel sizes 5 and 3) feed into a single-layer LSTM with hidden size 64; each convolution block applies \texttt{GroupNorm(num\_groups=min(4, C\_out))} and dropout. The model has \ModelParamCount{} trainable parameters. Before recurrent integration, the convolutional front end spans a 7-sample local temporal receptive field, approximately 27~ms at \TargetFsHz~Hz; the LSTM then integrates information across the full \WindowSec~s window.

The loss function is:

\[
\mathcal{L} = \mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{action}} + \lambda_f \, \mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{finger}}(\mathrm{non\text{-}REST}) + \lambda_a \, \mathrm{BCE}_{\mathrm{applicability}}
\]

where the finger loss is computed only on non-REST windows and the applicability loss is computed on all windows with target \(\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{action}\neq \mathrm{REST}]\). The action head additionally supports class weighting, including a configurable REST weight recorded in each run configuration.
For the featured run, \(\lambda_f=1.0\) and \(\lambda_a=0.5\), as recorded in \texttt{train\_config.json}; the action loss weight and REST action weight were both 1.0.

\subsubsection{Training Procedure}
Training hyperparameters are saved in \texttt{train\_config.json}. The featured 2-M16 configuration uses 60 epochs, batch size 64, learning rate 0.001, and Adam optimization, with the archived configuration lacking a recorded learning-rate scheduler, early stopping, and gradient clipping. These are therefore not reported.

Input normalization is performed with a per-channel normalizer fit on the training split. For the featured run, the saved normalizer records per-window centering and detrending before channel normalization. A held-out calibration subset of the training data is then used to fit separate scalar temperatures for the action, finger, and applicability heads, and those parameters are saved alongside the trained model.

\subsection{Calibration, Evaluation, and Actuation}
\subsubsection{Monte Carlo Dropout for Uncertainty Estimation}

Deterministic held-out evaluation, deployment-consistent decoding, pseudo-live replay, and Step~7 live inference all run the network in evaluation mode with dropout disabled. Dropout is activated only for the separate Step~3c MC-dropout diagnostic used to visualize uncertainty. For that diagnostic, dropout layers are deliberately activated for \(K\) stochastic forward passes:

\[
\mu = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{y}_k
\]

\[
\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\hat{y}_k - \mu)^2
\]

Reliability diagrams evaluate calibration, while confidence--uncertainty scatter plots visualize uncertainty concentration.

\subsubsection{Deterministic Evaluation and Calibration}
From each run's saved \texttt{test\_predictions.npz}, Davanzo computed ECE with the same convention as the evaluation pipeline: 10 equal-width confidence bins over $[0, 0.1], (0.1, 0.2], \ldots, (0.9, 1.0]$. In the archived predictions, softmax confidences are strictly positive, so assigning the exact boundary value 0.0 to the first bin has no practical effect. Table~\ref{tab:perf} reports action ECE over all test windows and finger ECE over true non-REST windows. The evaluation bundle also reports applicability false-positive and false-negative rates, action--applicability disagreement, and command-path invariants used to validate the deployment decoder.

For the featured run, action ECE is \FeaturedActionECE{} and finger ECE on true non-REST windows is \FeaturedFingerECE{}, indicating good agreement between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy.

\subsubsection{Online Inference and Actuation}
The command path is intentionally precision-biased: uncertain windows become no-send decisions before any best-guess actuation occurs. Postprocessing combines exponential smoothing, action/finger/applicability thresholds, a minimum joint-confidence requirement, a consecutive-window stability gate, and per-finger cooldown/hold intervals. In the featured March 19, 2026 bundle, commands require \FeaturedActuationStabilityWindows{} agreeing windows, equal to \FeaturedActuationStabilityMs{} ms at a \HopSec~s hop. Action, finger, and applicability thresholds are 0.05, 0.20, and 0.40.

The cooldown is an actuator-protection interval for the commanded finger, leaving other fingers free to receive commands if their own gates pass. The host serial ceiling is 20 commands/s, matching the 50 ms inference hop, and the Arduino receiver enforces per-finger hold timers with non-blocking servo updates. The action threshold is intentionally permissive; REST suppression is dominated by the REST/NONE invariant, finger and applicability gates, stability requirement, and cooldown.

When actuation is enabled, commands are sent over USB serial to an Arduino-linked five-finger robotic hand. Hardware safety gates prevent actuation whenever the predicted action is REST or the predicted finger is NONE, enforcing the same REST/NONE and OPEN-or-CLOSE/active-finger pairing used during deterministic evaluation.

Replay-time command-path summaries from the saved 2-M16 bundle form the auditable record for the results reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsubsection{Representative Run Selection and Reproducibility}
The project archive contains more recordings and runs than can be presented compactly. Quantitative reporting centers on the featured run because it includes archived windows, training configuration, deterministic evaluation, calibration review, and pseudo-live replay.

The March 19, 2026 2-M16 checkpoint was selected because the model-selection audit prioritized command-path safety---pseudo-live would-send precision, event hit rate, and false REST actuation---over offline split accuracy. A later checkpoint scored higher on some offline metrics, but its replay safety metrics were weaker.

The manuscript uses the archived window dataset, training configuration, summary metrics, calibrated predictions, replay output, and report figures for the featured 2-M16 run. The displayed training curve is a rerun of the featured recipe; the original deployment checkpoint predates per-epoch history logging.


\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This paper reports three levels of evaluation. First, held-out window metrics quantify action and finger separation under deterministic inference. Second, deployment-consistent decoding verifies that predictions remain valid after enforcing REST/NONE and active-finger invariants. Third, pseudo-live replay verifies that predictions survive the same stability gates, cooldowns, and command-suppression logic used before robotic actuation. Together, these levels establish the featured bundle's command-path readiness under the archived deployment protocol.

Table~\ref{tab:perf} reports the featured March 19, 2026 2-M16 deployment checkpoint. This run is the strongest complete evidence chain and anchors the analysis that follows. It reaches \FeaturedActionAcc\% action accuracy and \FeaturedFingerAcc\% deployment-consistent finger accuracy on \FeaturedNTest{} test windows (\FeaturedNNonRest{} true non-REST), well above uniform-prior chance levels of 33.3\% for three-way action classification and 20\% for five-way finger classification. The decisive result is that this performance survives the command logic required for actuation.

The paired deterministic evaluation and pseudo-live replay outputs inspect the full command path. Under deployment-style evaluation, the model reports \FeaturedJointAcc\% joint action--finger accuracy and \FeaturedReplayPrecision\% non-REST precision over \FeaturedReplayPositiveSendWindows{} emitted positive windows. Window-level send recall was \FeaturedReplayRecall\%, a deployment-throughput metric reflecting gate behavior, with classifier error captured separately in the held-out window metrics. It denotes \FeaturedReplayCorrectSendWindows{} correctly emitted non-REST windows out of \FeaturedReplayNonRestWindows{} true non-REST replay windows and reflects overlapping windows, stability gates, command persistence, duplicate suppression, and cooldown. At the event level, replay hit \FeaturedReplayEventHitRate\% of \FeaturedReplayEventCount{} movement events, with \FeaturedReplayEventMissCount{} misses and \FeaturedReplayLatencyMedianMs{} ms median first-command latency. Only \FeaturedReplayFalseActuationCount{} of \FeaturedReplayRestWindows{} true REST replay windows produced an actuation command, a false actuation rate of \FeaturedFalseActuationRateRest\%.

\subsection{Per-Class Behavior}
Per-class test accuracies and counts are derived from the saved per-window prediction bundles and reported separately for action and finger labels in Tables~\ref{tab:perclass-action} and~\ref{tab:perclass-finger}. The aggregate accuracy is distributed across classes, with no single class carrying the headline number. THUMB is near ceiling at 99.57\%, INDEX remains strong at 94.50\%, and PINKY reaches 89.57\%, while MIDDLE and RING are lower at 79.01\% and 65.48\%, respectively. RING is the main outlier at 65.48\%, and its lower accuracy is best explained by finger-specific representational overlap, low-density channel placement, and subject-specific signal geometry, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. This asymmetry matters because a single average finger-accuracy value obscures which classes require further development.

\subsection{Statistical Interpretation}
This paper reports 95\% confidence intervals (Wilson score) \cite{Wilson1927Inference} for action accuracy over $n_{test}$ windows and for finger accuracy over $n_{non\text{-}REST}$ windows in Table~\ref{tab:perf}. Chance levels reflect uniform priors and serve as reference baselines in the presence of class imbalance. These intervals reflect finite test-set uncertainty under an i.i.d.\ window assumption while omitting temporal correlation induced by overlapping windows and event segments, which narrows the effective sample size. Percentile bootstrap intervals computed from the same saved correctness vectors closely tracked the Wilson intervals and did not change the interpretation, so the main paper reports a single interval family.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
AlphaHand preserves finger-level decoding through actuation-grade command logic. The featured run reaches high offline accuracy, preserves \FeaturedJointAcc\% deterministic joint action--finger accuracy after deployment-style decoding, and keeps false REST actuation to \FeaturedFalseActuationRateRest\% under pseudo-live replay while hitting \FeaturedReplayEventHitRate\% of movement events. The deployment decoder reduces throughput, but the useful finger-level signal remains strong after the gates required for robotic-hand control.

The replay operating point favors precision over throughput. This is appropriate for an assistive-control command path because false movement can be more disruptive than missed movement. Precision is high (\FeaturedReplayPrecision\%), while window-level send recall is lower than held-out accuracy because the denominator counts overlapping windows, leaving intended-movement counts to the event-level hit rate. In practical terms, the current gate limits false movements by suppressing uncertain commands; in the featured replay stream, only \FeaturedReplayFalseActuationCount{} actuation-sent windows occurred on \FeaturedReplayRestWindows{} true REST windows.

The practical usability target is event-level hit rate with first-command latency: did each intended movement receive at least one correct command quickly enough to feel responsive? The current replay hit \FeaturedReplayEventHitRate\% of movement events with \FeaturedReplayLatencyMedianMs{} ms median and \FeaturedReplayLatencyPNinetyFiveMs{} ms p95 first-command latency. Threshold relaxation, shorter stability requirements, and cooldown tuning provide direct throughput controls, with precision and false-actuation tradeoffs measured directly in replay and live testing.

The per-finger breakdown reveals a second important asymmetry. THUMB, INDEX, and PINKY are strong in the featured run, while MIDDLE and RING remain less stable. RING is the clearest example of this asymmetry. Its lower accuracy is best explained by overlap between middle/ring motor representations, mechanically coupled finger movement during data collection, and the absence of central motor-strip electrodes that would better separate neighboring digits. With only TP9, AF7, AF8, and TP10, none of the recording sites lies over the central hand area typically sampled by C3/C4/Cz-style motor electrodes \cite{Bird2019MusePlacement}. The model is therefore decoding finger intent from a lateral frontal/temporal view of the sensorimotor system, operating without direct coverage of the central strip. The observed asymmetry reflects cortical representational overlap, limited channel coverage, and lower signal-to-noise ratio for some fingers, with classifier capacity as a secondary factor.

The channel ablation shows that the featured model relies primarily on temporal/periauricular information, with anterior-frontal channels contributing minimally to decoding performance. TP9 and TP10 are the most important electrodes by both leave-one-out and sufficiency criteria: removing either temporal electrode causes the largest action and finger losses, while the TP9+TP10 pair preserves most of the full-montage result. This shows where the trained classifier found the most useful subject-specific evidence in the available four-channel montage.

The comparison with Xiao and Ding's 39.7\% five-finger result is mechanistic, informing architecture without benchmarking performance. Their high-density study used a different task, feature family, and validation setting, while AlphaHand reports subject-specific, action-conditioned active-finger accuracy and then imposes REST/NONE command logic. The action/finger factorization, REST definition, and actuation gates change both the numerator and denominator. Xiao and Ding show that EEG can carry finger-specific spectral structure under rich sensing, while AlphaHand demonstrates that sufficient subject-specific structure remains in a sparse consumer montage to drive a precision-biased command path.

The next study phase will lock preprocessing, pruning, model-selection, calibration, and replay policies before data collection, then test command-path precision and event-level responsiveness across unfamiliar users and repeated sessions.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations bound the current evidence.

\textit{Statistical limitations.} Overlapping windows create temporal dependence, so Wilson intervals assume independent windows that the extraction procedure does not fully provide. Informative trial IDs are unavailable in the saved bundles, preventing trial-level aggregation and cleaner effective-sample-size estimates. The effective sample size is therefore likely smaller than the raw window count.

\textit{Dataset limitations.} Quantitative results are reported for one complete deployment evidence chain. The broader consented archive is more diverse, but those recordings do not yet provide matched datasets with trained checkpoints, calibration outputs, and replay outputs. The featured 2-M16 participant was a study-team self-subject, so familiarity with the task and recording protocol may inflate performance relative to users unfamiliar with the protocol. The featured dataset also uses targeted REST pruning, and the representative checkpoint was selected retrospectively from an archive using evidence-chain completeness and deployment safety. The channel ablations are single-seed, offline retraining studies; they provide subject-specific electrode-importance evidence, without informing anatomical localization. Prospective locked-protocol evaluation is needed before treating these numbers as unbiased multi-subject performance estimates.

\textit{Physiological and EEG artifact limitations.} Muse 2 lacks dedicated EMG/EOG channels, and no post-hoc ICA-based artifact rejection was available. Protocol-level artifact reduction and stream-health monitoring reduce but cannot fully rule out non-cortical artifacts such as movement, muscle, gaze, or connectivity artifacts. The low-density electrode placement also does not directly cover C3/C4/Cz motor areas, limiting anatomical specificity for neighboring fingers.

\textit{Deployment limitations.} Pseudo-live replay approximates live conditions, leaving full live-loop timing unvalidated. Dedicated live-loop latency logs are \NotAvailable{}; replay-time summaries therefore serve as provisional timing evidence pending a full end-to-end audit. User-level command utility has not yet been tested prospectively.

\section{Conclusion}

AlphaHand demonstrates that consumer EEG can support more than coarse grasp detection in a subject-specific setting. The strongest run reaches \FeaturedActionAcc\% held-out action accuracy and \FeaturedFingerAcc\% deployment-consistent finger accuracy, and the same bundle remains strong under deterministic evaluation and pseudo-live replay. The channel ablation further shows that, for this subject-specific split, TP9 and TP10 retained most of the full-montage decoding performance. This establishes an auditable end-to-end finger-level command-decoding path from a four-channel consumer headset. The next validation stage will test prospective multi-session performance with artifact-rejection logging, live-loop timing, false-actuation tracking, and user-level command-utility measures.
